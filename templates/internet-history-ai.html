Introduction

The internet is a large system of connected computer networks around the world, owned by different people, which allows them to share data with each other.

It grew out of research on packet switching in the 1960s, became a working network as ARPANET in 1969, and then kept expanding through new protocols, bigger backbones and global coordination.

This page follows the internet itself across six milestones, from the 1960s up to today: packet switching and ARPANET, TCP/IP, DNS, the NSFNET backbone and the commercialization of the network, global governance and IPv6, and the internet as it stands in 2026.

Events that belong to the World Wide Web are covered separately on the History of the Web page.

1. Packet Switching and ARPANET

Milestone: The first packet-switched network and its first four nodes

Year: 1961–1969

Event

In the early 1960s, Paul Baran at the RAND Corporation and Donald Davies at the UK National Physical Laboratory independently developed packet switching — breaking data into small blocks that are routed independently and reassembled at the destination — while Leonard Kleinrock’s research at MIT provided its mathematical basis. The US Advanced Research Projects Agency (ARPA) then funded the ARPANET: the first Interface Message Processor (IMP) was installed at UCLA in September 1969, the first host-to-host message was sent from UCLA to the Stanford Research Institute on October 29, 1969, and by the end of 1969 four nodes were connected at UCLA, SRI, UC Santa Barbara and the University of Utah.

Why it mattered

ARPANET was the first working packet-switched network and set the model the internet still uses today — many independent computers linked into one network that shares data without depending on a single central controller.

2. TCP/IP

Milestone: The “flag day” transition from NCP to TCP/IP

Year: 1974–1983

Event

Vinton Cerf and Robert Kahn published “A Protocol for Packet Network Intercommunication” in 1974, and the first specification of TCP appeared as RFC 675 in December 1974. The suite was later split into the Internet Protocol (RFC 791) and the Transmission Control Protocol (RFC 793), both published in September 1981. On January 1, 1983 — the “flag day” — every host on ARPANET was required to stop using the older Network Control Protocol (NCP) and switch to TCP/IP.

Why it mattered

This gave the internet its defining architecture — a “network of networks” that lets any TCP/IP-compliant system exchange data with any other, regardless of the underlying hardware. It remains the core protocol suite of the internet today.

3. DNS (Domain Name System)

Milestone: A distributed naming system replaces the single hosts.txt file

Year: 1983–1987

Event

Paul Mockapetris, at USC’s Information Sciences Institute, published RFC 882 and RFC 883 in November 1983, proposing a hierarchical, distributed system to replace the single manually-maintained hosts.txt file used to map computer names to numeric addresses. He built the first working name server, “Jeeves,” in 1983–84, and the system was formally standardized in RFCs 1034 and 1035 in November 1987.

Why it mattered

DNS let people use memorable names instead of numeric IP addresses and distributed the job of managing those names across many servers instead of one central authority — a design that scaled with the internet’s growth and remains its core naming infrastructure.

4. NSFNET and the Commercialization of the Internet

Milestone: The US research backbone, and the move to commercial operation

Year: 1986–1995

Event

To give researchers outside ARPANET access to shared computing, the National Science Foundation funded NSFNET, whose backbone came online in 1986 and connected regional networks and supercomputer centres. It was upgraded to T1 in 1988 and T3 in 1991, and it became the de facto US internet backbone, growing from about 2,000 connected computers in 1986 to more than two million by 1993. Once private carriers had built their own infrastructure, NSF decommissioned the NSFNET backbone on April 30, 1995 and the internet passed into commercial operation.

Why it mattered

NSFNET opened the network to the whole academic community and proved it could run at national scale. Handing it over to commercial carriers turned the internet from a publicly funded research project into infrastructure that businesses and the public could buy access to, which is what made its rapid growth possible.

5. Global Governance and IPv6

Milestone: Internet Society and ICANN, and the new version of IP

Year: 1992–2017

Event

As the network became worldwide, its coordination moved from US-funded projects to global, community-run bodies: the Internet Society was formed in 1992 to support the standards work of the IETF, and in November 1998 the US Department of Commerce recognized ICANN, a private non-profit corporation, to coordinate the internet’s names and numbers. Growth also exposed the limits of the IPv4 address space, so a new version of the protocol, IPv6, was specified in RFC 1883 in December 1995 and finally published as an Internet Standard in RFC 8200 in July 2017.

Why it mattered

Internet governance became a shared, international responsibility rather than a single government’s project, and IPv6 removed the long-term ceiling on how many devices the internet could address — a requirement for the mobile and connected devices that followed.

6. The Internet Today

Milestone: IANA stewardship transition and the internet at global scale

Year: 2016–2026

Event

On October 1, 2016, the stewardship of the IANA functions passed from the US government to the global internet community, completing the move to multistakeholder governance of the internet’s addressing and naming systems. In its Facts and Figures 2024 report, the International Telecommunication Union estimated that 5.5 billion people — 68 per cent of the world’s population — were using the internet, with 5G mobile networks covering about half of the world’s population.

Why it mattered

The internet is now critical infrastructure used by most of humanity, not a research network for a few institutions. The 2016 transition, and the addressing and mobile technology behind today’s scale, are what allow the network to keep growing without depending on any single country or organisation.
