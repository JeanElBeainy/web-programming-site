Introduction: the History of the Internet

The internet is a large system of connected computer networks that are spread around the world. The computers do not belong to a single entity, but are owned by different people which allows them to share data with each other. It grew out of research on packet switching in the 1960s and became a working network as ARPANET in 1969, and then kept expanding through new protocols, bigger backbones and global coordination until it reached billions of people today.

1. Packet Switching and ARPANET

Event: In the early 1960s, Paul Baran at the RAND Corporation and Donald Davies at the UK National Physical Laboratory independently developed packet switching — breaking data into small blocks that are routed independently and reassembled at the destination — while Leonard Kleinrock's research at MIT provided its mathematical basis. The US Advanced Research Projects Agency (ARPA) then funded the ARPANET: the first Interface Message Processor (IMP) was installed at UCLA in September 1969, the first host-to-host message was sent from UCLA to the Stanford Research Institute on October 29, 1969, and by the end of 1969 four nodes were connected at UCLA, SRI, UC Santa Barbara and the University of Utah.

Why it mattered: ARPANET was the first working packet-switched network and set the model the internet still uses today — many independent computers linked into one network that shares data without depending on a single central controller.

Sources:

  • B. M. Leiner, V. G. Cerf, D. D. Clark, R. E. Kahn, L. Kleinrock, D. C. Lynch, J. Postel, L. G. Roberts, and S. Wolff, "A Brief History of the Internet," Internet Society.
  • Internet Hall of Fame, "Internet History Timeline," Internet Society.
  • National Science Foundation, "The Internet," NSF: Nifty 50.

2. TCP/IP: The "Flag Day" Protocol Transition

Event: Vinton Cerf and Robert Kahn published "A Protocol for Packet Network Intercommunication" in 1974, and the first specification of TCP appeared as RFC 675 in December 1974. The suite was later split into the Internet Protocol (RFC 791) and the Transmission Control Protocol (RFC 793), both published in September 1981. On January 1, 1983 — the "flag day" — every host on ARPANET was required to stop using the older Network Control Protocol (NCP) and switch to TCP/IP.

Why it mattered: This gave the internet its defining architecture — a "network of networks" that lets any TCP/IP-compliant system exchange data with any other, regardless of the underlying hardware. It remains the core protocol suite of the internet today.

Sources:

  • V. Cerf, Y. Dalal, and C. Sunshine, "Specification of Internet Transmission Control Program," RFC 675, Internet Engineering Task Force, Dec. 1974.
  • J. Postel, "Internet Protocol," RFC 791, Internet Engineering Task Force, Sep. 1981.
  • J. Postel, "Transmission Control Protocol," RFC 793, Internet Engineering Task Force, Sep. 1981.

3. DNS: Introduction of a Distributed Naming Architecture

Event: Paul Mockapetris, at USC's Information Sciences Institute, published RFC 882 and RFC 883 in November 1983, proposing a hierarchical, distributed system to replace the single manually-maintained hosts.txt file used to map computer names to numeric addresses. He built the first working name server, "Jeeves," in 1983–84, and the system was formally standardized in RFCs 1034 and 1035 in November 1987.

Why it mattered: DNS let people use memorable names instead of numeric IP addresses and distributed the job of managing those names across many servers instead of one central authority — a design that scaled with the internet's growth and remains its core naming infrastructure.

Sources:

  • P. Mockapetris, "Domain Names — Concepts and Facilities," RFC 1034, Internet Engineering Task Force, Nov. 1987.
  • P. Mockapetris, "Domain Names — Implementation and Specification," RFC 1035, Internet Engineering Task Force, Nov. 1987.
  • Internet Hall of Fame, "Paul Mockapetris," Internet Society.

4. NSFNET and the Commercialization of the Internet

Event: To give researchers outside ARPANET access to shared computing, the National Science Foundation funded NSFNET, whose backbone came online in 1986 and connected regional networks and supercomputer centres. It was upgraded to T1 in 1988 and T3 in 1991, and it became the de facto US internet backbone, growing from about 2,000 connected computers in 1986 to more than two million by 1993. Once private carriers had built their own infrastructure, NSF decommissioned the NSFNET backbone on April 30, 1995 and the internet passed into commercial operation.

Why it mattered: NSFNET opened the network to the whole academic community and proved it could run at national scale. Handing it over to commercial carriers turned the internet from a publicly funded research project into the infrastructure that businesses and the public could buy access to, which is what made its rapid growth possible.

Sources:

  • National Science Foundation, "The Internet," NSF: Nifty 50.
  • National Science Foundation, "Birth of the Commercial Internet," NSF Impacts.
  • Internet Society, "A Brief History of the Internet."

5. Global Governance and IPv6

Event: As the network became worldwide, its coordination moved from US-funded projects to global, community-run bodies: the Internet Society was formed in 1992 to support the standards work of the IETF, and in November 1998 the US Department of Commerce recognized ICANN, a private non-profit corporation, to coordinate the internet's names and numbers. Growth also exposed the limits of the IPv4 address space, so a new version of the protocol, IPv6, was specified in RFC 1883 in December 1995 and finally published as an Internet Standard in RFC 8200 in July 2017.

Why it mattered: Internet governance became a shared, international responsibility rather than a single government's project, and IPv6 removed the long-term ceiling on how many devices the internet could address — a requirement for the mobile and connected devices that followed.

Sources:

  • Internet Society, "Our History."
  • ICANN, "A Brief History of ICANN."
  • S. Deering and R. Hinden, "Internet Protocol, Version 6 (IPv6) Specification," RFC 8200, Internet Engineering Task Force, Jul. 2017.

6. The Internet Today

Event: On October 1, 2016, the stewardship of the IANA functions passed from the US government to the global internet community, completing the move to multistakeholder governance of the internet's addressing and naming systems. In its Facts and Figures 2024 report, the International Telecommunication Union estimated that 5.5 billion people — 68 per cent of the world's population — were using the internet, with 5G mobile networks covering about half of the world's population.

Why it mattered: The internet is now critical infrastructure used by most of humanity, not a research network for a few institutions. The 2016 transition, and the addressing and mobile technology behind today's scale, are what allow the network to keep growing without depending on any single country or organisation.

Sources:

  • ICANN, "Stewardship and Accountability."
  • International Telecommunication Union, "Connectivity and 5G coverage increase worldwide; one-third of population remains offline, mainly in rural areas," ITU Facts and Figures 2024, Nov. 27, 2024.
  • Internet Society, "A Brief History of the Internet."
