Introduction

The World Wide Web, or simply called the Web, is a system of documents that are linked together and can be accessed through the internet. It is not similar to the internet; the internet carries the data of the web which we find and share information on.

It all began between 1989 and 1991 when Tim Berners-Lee proposed a way for scientists to link their documents together in different computers without the need to exchange them manually. For this to be achievable, he created HTML to format the pages and HTTP to transfer them.

From this point, it started to expand and have seen multiple milestones such as the release of Mosaic browser in 1993 which made the web easy to access, and the founding of Google in 1998.

1. World Wide Web — Invention at CERN

Milestone: Creation of HTML, HTTP, and the URL

Year: 1989–1991

Event

Tim Berners-Lee, a researcher at CERN, proposed a hypertext-based system for linking documents in March 1989. By late 1990, with Robert Cailliau, he had built HTML, URLs, and HTTP, plus the first browser/editor and web server. The first website went live on August 6, 1991.

Why it mattered

The web is the application layer that made the internet browsable and usable by non-specialists, turning a network for technical users into a system of interlinked, navigable documents.

2. Mosaic — The First Popular Web Browser

Milestone: Public release of NCSA Mosaic

Year: 1993

Event

Released January 23, 1993, by Marc Andreessen and Eric Bina at the University of Illinois’s National Center for Supercomputing Applications (NCSA), Mosaic was the first browser to display images inline with text and was distributed free, with versions for Windows, Macintosh, and Unix.

Why it mattered

Mosaic’s ease of use is widely credited with turning the web from an academic tool into something ordinary people could use, setting the visual and navigational template for every browser that followed.

3. Google — Organizing the Web at Scale

Milestone: Founding of Google Inc.

Year: 1998

Event

Larry Page and Sergey Brin, Stanford PhD students, developed a search engine (“BackRub”) that ranked pages by analyzing links pointing to them, rather than keyword frequency alone. They incorporated Google Inc. on September 4, 1998.

Why it mattered

Google’s link-based ranking made the rapidly growing web navigable and searchable at scale, becoming the dominant way people found information online and shaping how the web’s content economy developed.
